# Chewy thoughts: hybrid cars are not stepping stones

When people argue about cars they usually pick a side: internal combustion or electric. Hybrids barely show up. I think that is a category error. For a lot of driving, on the roads and fueling networks we actually have, hybrid cars are not stepping stones on the way to a battery car. They are the better car.

I mean two different machines, and mixing them is how the argument gets sloppy. A self-charging hybrid (HEV) never plugs in. Its battery is a working buffer: it recaptures braking, covers the engine's worst moments, and lets the engine shut off in traffic. A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) adds a much bigger battery and a cord. Most weekdays can be electric. A long day still ends at a gas pump. I have a charger and solar, so the PHEV is the car I am actually describing when I say this is not a layover. The HEV is the version that still wins if you never plug anything in. Those can both be right without being the same claim.

The idea I keep coming back to is this: people count the extra hardware as waste. Two powertrains, extra weight, extra complexity. Sometimes that is fair. An unplugged PHEV is a heavy hybrid. A battery-electric with 300 miles of cells, used for a 40-mile commute, is carrying insurance it will not spend. The interesting design is the one where the extra stuff is doing work.

##### Aren't hybrids more mechanically complex?

This is the objection I hear most, and it always felt strange. I am not a mechanic. Why would "more parts" matter unless it shows up as breakdowns or bills?

"More complex" is two different claims. One is that more things fail. The other is that when something fails it is expensive and few shops will touch it. People picture two complete cars stacked on one chassis. That is the wrong picture.

An HEV is not an engine plus an EV. It is a system that *deletes* parts and wear. A motor-generator already makes electricity, so a lot of hybrids do not need a conventional alternator. The motor can spin the engine up instead of a starter, which is why cold-start wear is lower, not because the engine magically got nicer. Regenerative braking means pads last much longer. The engine runs fewer hours, and more often in a band it likes. You are not paying for two of everything. You are paying for a small battery that lets the engine stop doing the jobs it is bad at.

A PHEV inherits some of that, but its real synergy is division of labor across days, not deleted ICE parts. Most days are short. That is the job a battery is good at. Some days are long, cold, or far from a charger. That is the job a fuel tank is good at. Two systems doing the same job twice is waste. Two systems doing different jobs is a Swiss Army knife. You still have to admit the knife is heavier, costlier, and annoying if the local shop will not open it. You cannot hand-wave that with "it doesn't even have an alternator."

The fleet numbers split the same way the two claims do.

Argonne National Lab's 2021 total-cost study, using service schedules rather than vibes, put scheduled maintenance at about $0.101 per mile for a gas car, $0.094 for an HEV, $0.090 for a PHEV, and $0.061 for a battery-electric. That is 7%, 11%, and 41% less than gas. Consumer Reports, using owner surveys of actual repair and maintenance spend over 200,000 miles, found gas at about $0.061 per mile and PHEVs and BEVs at about $0.030–$0.031. On money out of pocket, the plug-in hybrid lands with the EV, at roughly half of gas. Argonne's Edmunds slice had the Prius as the cheapest model in the whole set.

Consumer Reports' reliability survey — problems reported, not dollars — goes the other way. Hybrids have about 15% *fewer* problems than gas-only cars. PHEVs and battery-electrics have about 80% *more*. That is not a contradiction. HEV complexity is old, mostly Toyota and Honda, and it reduces wear. PHEV complexity looks like a new EV (charging hardware, a bigger pack, software, often a new platform) plus an engine. The invoices mechanics actually flinch at are the inverter and the high-voltage pack, often a few thousand dollars when they happen. Toyota hybrid packs commonly last well past 150,000 miles. The routine work — oil, pads, engine hours — is the part that got quieter.

So complexity matters if it shows up. For HEVs, the evidence says it mostly does not. For PHEVs, you are cheaper to service than gas and more likely to have *a* problem than gas, at least while the designs are young. "Two systems" is a fairer insult to a PHEV than to a Prius. It is still not the insult people think it is.

##### Aren't hybrids heavier because they have two systems?

Yes, versus the gas twin. No, as a reason the EV wins.

A 2024 gas RAV4 is about 3,370 pounds. The Hybrid is about 3,690–3,730. The Prime is about 4,230–4,300. Tesla's Model Y, a reasonable EV to compare that SUV with, is about 4,150–4,400. The Prime is 500-odd pounds heavier than the Hybrid and 800–900 heavier than gas. It is not some unique lard-ball next to a battery car. You swapped mass.

The packs tell you why. An HEV battery is a buffer: about 1.5–2 kWh on a RAV4 Hybrid. A PHEV pack is a commute tank: 13.6 kWh in a Prius Prime, 18.1 in a RAV4 Prime, a bit over 22 in the newer one. A long-range EV pack is typically 60–80 kWh. The PHEV battery is roughly a quarter to a fifth of the EV's. Packs run something like 5–7 kg per kWh, so a 75 kWh EV pack is on the order of 450–500 kg sitting in the floor. The Prime's pack is only on the order of 120 kg heavier than the Hybrid's tiny one, despite about ten times the energy. The rest of the Prime's extra weight is motors, structure, and still having an engine.

Electric *range* shrinks more than kilowatt-hours, because you are still hauling that engine. A PHEV is typically 40–50 electric miles. A BEV is typically 250–350. That is about one-sixth to one-eighth the electric range, not one-quarter. You are not trying to match 300 electric miles. You are trying to cover a commute. Forty electric miles is in the right band for a lot of American daily driving. Total range with the tank is about 600 miles, which is more than the EV, not less.

The "smaller gas tank" half of the objection is often overstated. The gas RAV4, the Hybrid, and the Prime all have 14.5 gallons. Some PHEVs shave the tank. Many do not. Cargo space is the more honest packaging cost.

Does the extra mass make it less efficient? Some. Heavier means more energy to get moving and more rolling resistance. A rough rule of thumb is on the order of 1% more energy per 100 pounds, worse in stop-go, milder on the highway. Regenerative braking claws back some of the city penalty, which is why hybrids are less punished for weight than a naive gas car would be. They do not get a free pass. You can see the tax on the sticker: the RAV4 Hybrid is about 40 mpg combined; the Prime, running as a hybrid with the plug-in pack empty, is about 38. That 2 mpg is the bigger battery showing up as weight. Electric miles are thirstier too. You dragged an engine the Tesla left at the factory.

That does not reverse the HEV result. The Hybrid is already hundreds of pounds heavier than the gas RAV4 and still crushes it on fuel. The synergy is larger than the mass penalty. Weight is a real cost. It is not the deciding cost.

Against a battery car, the comparison is less black and white, and that is the true shape of it. In the SUV class they often weigh about the same. You did not escape weight by shrinking the battery. You swapped a few hundred kilograms of cells for an engine, a tank, and an exhaust. Efficiency then depends which day it is. On a commute you can cover electrically, the PHEV is dragging an unused engine and the EV wins on watt-hours per mile. On a day that needs 250 miles, the EV is dragging 200 miles of cells you are about to cook off at a stall, and the PHEV is a slightly chubby hybrid with a pump.

Everyone pays for mass. The question is whether that mass is doing work on the day you are having. HEV mass is a small buffer that earns its keep most of the time. PHEV mass is a commute battery plus a road-trip engine: redundant on any single day, useful across a year. BEV mass is a 300-mile tank you carry on 40-mile days. An unplugged PHEV bought the extra pack and is not using it, which is the version of this objection that sticks.

##### Isn't range anxiety just an EV growing pain?

It is a real problem, and it is mostly a public, on-the-road problem.

A US gas fill is about five minutes for 300–400 miles, at something like 145,000–150,000 stations. Public charging is not that. As of early 2025 the US had on the order of 200,000 public charging ports, but about three-quarters were Level 2 — hours, not minutes. DC fast stalls were in the 50,000–65,000 range. A fast charge is typically 20–60 minutes to about 80%, and you still have to find a stall that works.

Home charging deletes the daily version. I can plug in overnight and send solar into the car, so weekday PHEV miles can be electric without a public stall. An HEV never gets that solar-to-wheels path. A battery car still has the road-trip version: plan, wait, hope. The PHEV's answer to range anxiety is not a bigger pack. It is that empty-battery mode is still a car.

If you do not plug the PHEV in, this whole section collapses. You bought EV hardware and are using it as a heavy hybrid. ICCT's European data is the steelman: real-world PHEV fuel use about three to five times the official sticker; private cars maybe half their miles electric, company cars more like 10–15%, against test cycles that assumed something like 70–85%. That is why policymakers soured on PHEVs. The honest claim is narrower than "hybrids are just better." If you can charge overnight, a PHEV takes the EV's daily win and the gas car's long-distance win without asking the rest of the country to rebuild the curb. If you cannot or will not, you wanted an HEV.

##### Why aren't hybrids more popular, then?

If the product is this good, the conversation should not be a two-sided war. I think popularity is a different question from "which car is better," and it has a few mechanisms that should stay distinct.

The narrative is the deepest one. A battery car feels like a destination. A hybrid feels like a layover. Once a technology is filed under "transition," buying it feels like being left behind even if it is the better car *now*. The Prius already had its cultural moment as a virtue object. Tesla made EVs fast and expensive-looking instead of dutiful. PHEVs have it worse: they are *designed* as a both, and the culture can only respect an either.

Policy is the blunt one. A lot of countries only subsidize zero-tailpipe vehicles. The honest steelman is that they are not trying to pick the best car on the lot. They are trying to kill the tailpipe, build a battery industry, and avoid subsidizing the thing they are trying to leave. Hybrids still use oil. They are also a Japanese strength. EVs were a chance to leapfrog. There is a meaner version that is probably also true: if the goal is tons of CO2 moved per subsidy dollar, hybrids might move more people off gas cars, because they are cheaper and an HEV does not need a charger. Policy is optimizing for the ladder, not for substitution. PHEVs often *did* get the subsidies, then got kicked off for looking like fake EVs on paper. That is the ICCT story becoming industrial policy.

China is supply-side industrial policy, not a consumer-product verdict. Cheap cells and cheap EVs can make battery-electric look like "the" technology even if the median driver still does not have a place to plug in. It is also a worse explanation of hybrid unpopularity than it first appears. BYD's plug-in hybrids are a mass-market success. The thing being ignored in American car talk is not being ignored everywhere.

Tesla belongs under the narrative, not in a pile with Beijing. Great marketing changed what an electric car was allowed to look like. It did not make a charger appear on your street.

##### What would have to be true

If I am wrong about HEVs, it will show up as the complexity story finally winning: inverters and packs that fail young, shops that cannot work on them, maintenance that actually costs more than gas. The current reliability surveys say the opposite.

If I am wrong about PHEVs, it will be because people do not plug them in, or because the extra problems never get designed out. A PHEV whose owner never charges is a worse HEV. A PHEV whose charging hardware and software keep breaking is a worse EV plus a worse engine. Either one can be true for a given model year without making the *design* a mistake.

Battery cars win more of the map if charging becomes equivalent to refueling for people who do not own the curb: stalls as assumed as streetlights, charge times that are a bathroom break, winter range that is boring, apartments that are not a veto. Some of that is happening. All of it at once, for the median driver, is a different world than the one in which you currently buy a car.

Until then, the gas car wastes energy in ordinary driving. The battery car imports a fueling problem into ordinary life unless your housing already solved it. The HEV is the machine that noticed the first fact and does not care about the second. The PHEV is the machine that noticed both, if you have somewhere to plug in.

I do. That is why I am not waiting for the destination. For me it is a plug-in hybrid I actually charge, with a self-charging hybrid as the answer for everyone who will not.
